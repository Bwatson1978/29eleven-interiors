Why the rooms that feel the best rarely look the most impressive in photos

Ashley Porter Principal Designer, 29 Eleven Interiors

There's a particular kind of room that photographs beautifully + feels wrong the moment you step into it. The proportion is right, the palette is right, every piece is from the right place — + yet. You stand there + something is missing. This is the gap between design that performs + design that lives, + it's the thing we think about most.

I've been designing spaces professionally since 2010, + somewhere in the middle of those fifteen years I started noticing a pattern: the projects that generated the most buzz on social media were rarely the ones clients emailed about years later. The ones clients emailed about — sometimes five or six years after the project wrapped, always unprompted — were the quieter ones. The ones where nothing was trying especially hard.

What separates them? I've been thinking about this for a long time + I think it comes down to one thing: whether the room was designed for a viewer or for an inhabitant.

The camera flattens everything — including the lies

Photography is a ruthless editor. It collapses depth, neutralizes smell, eliminates sound, + removes the feeling of scale that your body understands the moment it enters a room. What remains is geometry + light. + geometry + light photograph beautifully even when they produce nothing of particular feeling.

This is why you can look at a perfectly staged model home + feel nothing. Every proportion is correct. Every finish is considered. But it was designed for the photograph — or worse, for the sale — not for the life that would eventually fill it. The camera can't tell the difference. But you can. The moment you walk through the door, your body starts taking readings that no lens captures: the temperature of the light, the give of the rug underfoot, whether the sofa is the right distance from everything else for a person to feel settled rather than perched.

The best rooms I've ever stood in were almost always photographed badly. The worst rooms I've ever stood in were almost always photographed beautifully.

— Ashley Porter

The best rooms I've ever stood in were almost always photographed badly. Not because they were ugly — they weren't — but because the things that made them extraordinary were invisible to the camera. The heft of the curtains. The way the afternoon light didn't glare but dissolved. The scent of the wood, faintly. The specific rightness of a chair that was chosen not for how it looked in a layout but for how long a person could sit in it before they wanted to move.

What the body knows that the eye doesn't

There's a concept in architecture called haptic design — designing for the sense of touch as well as sight. It sounds esoteric, but in practice it's quite simple: it means paying attention to everything your body registers about a space that your eye doesn't consciously process. The temperature differential between a stone floor + a wood one. The acoustic deadening of heavy textiles. The barely perceptible give in a sofa cushion that tells you this is a place to stay.

Most residential design ignores this almost entirely. The industry runs on images. We sell projects through images, we get clients through images, we win awards through images. So naturally, design has gradually bent toward what photographs well. Glossier surfaces. Higher contrast. Fewer layers. Less warmth. More drama.

Harbor Cove, 2022 · Photo: Molly Rose Photography

What gets lost in this drift is texture — not just the physical texture of materials but the conceptual texture of a room. The sense that things were gathered rather than installed. That the room has a past, even if it was finished last year. That somebody actually lives here.

From the Studio

When we're evaluating a room during a project, we have a habit of standing in it + closing our eyes for thirty seconds. What you notice in those thirty seconds — what sounds reach you, whether the air feels weighted or hollow, whether your body wants to walk forward or stop — tells you more about how a space is working than any set of photos.

Designing for the inhabitant, not the audience

The shift in approach is subtle but it changes every decision. Instead of asking "how does this look?" you start asking "how does this feel after the third week?" Instead of optimizing for the wide shot, you optimize for the close-up — the grain of the wood on the tabletop, the weight of the throw, whether the reading chair has a surface nearby to set a glass on without having to think about it.

It means accepting that some of the most important decisions in a room are invisible in photographs. The underlay beneath the rug. The quality of the drawer hardware — not how it looks but how it sounds when it closes. The lumbar support of a chair that will be sat in for the next twenty years. These things don't show up on Instagram. But they are, in a very real sense, the whole point.

When I think about the Harbor Cove project — the one that gets the most attention when I share photos — the things I'm most proud of are all invisible in those images. The acoustic panels embedded behind the walls so the great room doesn't echo. The specific height of the kitchen island that suits this particular family's particular height. The way the primary bedroom sits on the house so that the light comes in at seven in the morning + wakes you gently rather than abruptly.

None of that is in any photograph. But the clients have sent me three unprompted emails in the two years since we finished, + every one of them mentions not how the house looks but how it feels. How they don't want to leave it. How they didn't understand what a home could feel like until they lived in this one.

That is the whole ambition. Not the image — the feeling. The photograph is just the proof of concept.
